\section{Adaptive-Scale Vector Quantization}
In this section, we first motivate adaptive scaling as a just-enough reparameterization for efficient codebook learning, and then present the formal definition of ASVQ.

\subsection{Nearest-Neighbor Assignment}
A central question behind reparameterization-based VQ methods is which part of the added expressiveness is essential for improving codebook learning. We first examine how the nearest-neighbor assignment rule responds to feature statistics and codeword geometry. To see this, only consider two distinct codewords $e_i$ and $e_j$. A sample $z$ is assigned to codeword $i$ over $j$ if and only if the squared-distance difference $\Delta_{ij}(z) < 0$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    \Delta_{ij}(z)
    &\coloneqq \|z-e_i\|_2^2-\|z-e_j\|_2^2 \\
    &= 2z^\top(e_j-e_i)+\|e_i\|_2^2-\|e_j\|_2^2 .
\end{split}
\label{eq:main_dist_diff}
\end{equation}
Denote the codeword difference by $\delta_{ij}=e_j-e_i$ and the feature covariance by $\Sigma=\operatorname{Cov}(z)$. Since the last two terms in Eq.~\eqref{eq:main_dist_diff} are constants with respect to $z$, the variance of the assignment variable is
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Var}(\Delta_{ij})
    =
    4\delta_{ij}^{\top}\Sigma\delta_{ij}.
\label{eq:main_assignment_var}
\end{equation}
This expression shows that assignment sensitivity depends jointly on the covariance structure of latent features and the alignment of codeword differences with high-variance directions. If $\Sigma=U\Lambda U^\top$, then
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Var}(\Delta_{ij})
    =
    4\sum_{r=1}^{D}\lambda_r (u_r^\top \delta_{ij})^2 ,
\label{eq:main_eigen_assignment_var}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_r$ and $u_r$ are the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $\Sigma$. Hence, a high-variance direction contributes strongly to the assignment when the codeword difference has a non-negligible projection onto that direction. Conversely, if $\delta_{ij}$ is orthogonal to a high-variance direction, that direction does not affect the assignment between this pair of codewords.

The same scale-sensitive effect also appears in codebook updates. Under a fixed-assignment view, the per-sample gradient of the standard codebook loss with respect to an assigned codeword is proportional to the residual between the codeword and the assigned feature. As shown in Appendix~\ref{sec:app_gradient_variance}, for channel $d$,
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Var}(g_{k,d}\mid q(z)=k)
    =
    4\operatorname{Var}(z_d\mid q(z)=k).
\label{eq:gradient_var_main}
\end{equation}
Thus, after assignments are made, the variability of codeword updates is also controlled by the conditional feature scale within each Voronoi region \cite{VoronoiNouvellesAD}. Together with assignment frequency, this affects the effective update signal received by each codeword. This analysis is local and does not claim to fully characterize the discontinuous dynamics of VQ training, but it identifies a concrete scale-dependent component in both assignment and update behavior.

\subsection{Scale Coupling}
The previous subsection isolates a common factor behind both assignment and update behavior: channel-wise feature scale changes the effective weighting of distances before assignment and the amplitude of updates after assignment. This suggests that scale is not merely a descriptive statistic of the latent space, but a quantity that the codebook must implicitly track to cover the data manifold effectively during training. We now explain why absorbing this role directly into $e_k$ creates a concrete optimization difficulty in standard VQ.

For analysis, define the channel-wise scale of the current codebook by
\begin{equation}
    c \coloneqq \sigma(\mathcal{E}) \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}^D,
    \qquad
    \tilde{e}_k \coloneqq e_k \oslash c,
\label{eq:codebook_scale_factorization}
\end{equation}
where $\oslash$ denotes element-wise division. Then each codeword can be written as $e_k = c \odot \tilde{e}_k$. This is only an analytical decomposition rather than a new model parameterization: $\{\tilde{e}_k\}_{k=1}^K$ captures the scale-normalized relative geometry of the codebook, while $c$ captures the global per-channel magnitude shared across codewords.

Let $\tilde{\delta}_{ij} \coloneqq \tilde{e}_j-\tilde{e}_i$. Substituting $\delta_{ij}=c\odot \tilde{\delta}_{ij}$ into Eq.~\eqref{eq:main_assignment_var} gives
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Var}(\Delta_{ij})
    =
    4\tilde{\delta}_{ij}^{\top}\operatorname{diag}(c)\,\Sigma\,\operatorname{diag}(c)\tilde{\delta}_{ij}.
\label{eq:assignment_var_scale_coupling}
\end{equation}
Therefore, channel scale acts as a diagonal weighting on the same scale-normalized geometry $\tilde{\delta}_{ij}$: even when the relative geometry remains appropriate, changes in feature scale can still distort the effective competition between codewords by reweighting channels unequally.

In standard VQ, however, the same parameter $e_k$ must simultaneously serve two roles. Through $\tilde{e}_k$, it determines the relative geometry that defines Voronoi boundaries; through $c$, it must also track the global channel scale of encoder features. When encoder statistics drift, adapting the scale through updates to $e_k$ inevitably perturbs $\tilde{e}_k$ as well, and hence shifts codeword competition. Conversely, changing geometry also changes the effective channel weighting inherited from $c$. We refer to this entanglement as \emph{scale coupling}. In other words, the codebook is forced to use one set of parameters to solve two different problems: organizing codeword geometry and following feature-scale drift.

This diagnosis also clarifies what kind of reparameterization is actually necessary. The instability identified above enters through channel-wise magnitudes, not through missing translations, rotations, dense channel mixing, or nonlinear transformations. Therefore, the minimal intervention is to separate these two roles: keep a normalized codebook to learn relative geometry, and introduce an explicit channel-wise scale variable to absorb feature-scale drift. In this sense, adaptive scale is \emph{just enough}: it directly targets the scale-dependent factor exposed by Eqs.~\eqref{eq:main_assignment_var}, \eqref{eq:gradient_var_main}, and \eqref{eq:assignment_var_scale_coupling}, while avoiding additional degrees of freedom that are not required by the analysis.

\subsection{ASVQ Formulation}

Motivated by the diagnosis above, we introduce Adaptive-Scale Vector Quantization (ASVQ). ASVQ preserves nearest-neighbor quantization and the standard VQ training objective, while explicitly decoupling codeword structure from channel scale through a lightweight reparameterization.

The effective codebook of ASVQ, \(\hat{\mathcal{E}}=\{\hat{e}_k\}_{k=1}^K\), is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\hat{e}_k = s \odot \tilde{e}_k
\label{eq:e_k_hat}
\end{equation}
where \(\tilde{e}_k \in \mathbb{R}^D\) is the normalized base codeword, \(s \in \mathbb{R}^D\) is the global channel scaling factor, and \(\odot\) denotes element-wise multiplication. The quantization assignment uses \(\hat{e}_k\):
\begin{equation}
q(z)=\arg\min_k \|z-\hat{e}_k\|_2^2, \qquad z_q=\hat{e}_{q(z)}
\label{eq:quantization_assignment}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Codebook Normalization: Constraining Channel Scale Degrees of Freedom}
We introduce channel-wise scale normalization for the base codewords. Given the codebook parameters \(\mathcal{E}=\{e_k\}_{k=1}^K\), we define the mean and standard deviation of each channel across the codebook as:
\begin{equation}
\mu_d = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^K e_{j,d}, \qquad \sigma(\mathcal{E})_d = \sqrt{\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^K (e_{j,d}-\mu_d)^2}
\label{eq:mean_std_stats}
\end{equation}
The base codewords are constructed accordingly:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{e}_k = \frac{e_k}{\sigma(\mathcal{E})}
\label{eq:norm_e_tilde}
\end{equation}
This normalization constrains the overall expansion of the codebook across channels within a controllable range, reducing the channel scale drift inherent in the codebook during training. Consequently, relative structural differences between codewords are primarily expressed through \(\tilde{e}_k\), rather than relying on magnitude expansion in a subset of channels to achieve distance advantages.

\subsubsection{Global Channel Scaling Factor: Tracking Input Feature Scale Variations}

Moreover, we introduce a global learnable scaling factor \(s\), to track the channel scales of \(z\), ensuring that the channel scales of the effective codewords match their features. Ideally, \(s_d\) should be of the same order as the standard deviation of \(z_d\).

In terms of distance representation:
\begin{equation}
\|z - s \odot \tilde{e}_k\|_2^2 = \sum_{d=1}^D s_d^2\left(\frac{z_d}{s_d}-\tilde{e}_{k,d}\right)^2
\label{eq:weighted_euclidean_dist}
\end{equation}
This formulation demonstrates that \(s\) explicitly incorporates input channel scale variations into the quantization process. When the channel scales of \(z\) drift during training, the codewords do not need to passively follow these changes, thereby alleviating the coupling pressure.

To obtain stable and low-overhead scale estimates, we suggest to employ an Exponential Moving Average to update \(s\) in practice:
\begin{equation}
s^{(t)} \leftarrow \gamma s^{(t-1)} + (1-\gamma) \sigma(z^{(t)})
\label{eq:state_update_smooth}
\end{equation}
where \(\sigma(z^{(t)})\) is the channel-wise standard deviation vector of the current batch and \(\gamma \in (0,1)\) is the smoothing coefficient. This update method does not rely on gradient optimization and can smoothly track feature scale variations during training, thereby enhancing stability.
